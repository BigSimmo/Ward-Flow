**A question that mentions cost at all has already answered itself** for any respondent who is not
deliberately resisting it. Naming the status quo and pricing the alternative is an argument, not a
neutral choice, even when both figures are true.

**Observed twice in twenty minutes, 2026-09-04, on the same question** — what to call the thing on
every panel of a page. An open ask from one chat ("what do you call this out loud?") got **"Journey"**
in one word, volunteered. Two loaded asks got **"Movement"**:

- Mine offered four options and described Journey entirely in terms of what changing would COST,
  while Movement opened with _"No work."_
- The other chat's follow-up went further: it named a default, attached _"changes nothing"_ to it,
  and **offered silence as the route to it** — the respondent did not even have to choose the cheap
  option, it was the outcome of not answering.

Neither of us set out to steer him. We both did it **by being helpful about effort**.

⚠️ **The real cost is not the wrong word — it is suppressing knowledge only the user has.** "What
would you call this?" is a question a psychiatrist can answer and I cannot. Replacing it with "keep
it or change it, and here is the price" is a question anyone can answer, and it throws away the
reason for asking.

⚠️ **Confirmation questions are where this gets in.** They are written in a hurry, after the
interesting thinking is done, when the goal has quietly become "close this out". The other chat's
FIRST ask was clean and its follow-up was worse — and it wrote the bad one within minutes of
diagnosing the identical defect in mine.

**How to apply.** For anything where the user's own vocabulary, clinical judgement or preference is
the point: ask open, no options, no prices. Mention effort only if asked, or after the answer.
Reserve `AskUserQuestion` with options for choices that are genuinely mine to cost — approach,
sequencing, scope — never for what a thing is called or what matters clinically. If a decision
arrives via another chat, quote the question that produced it: **the answer belongs to the
question**, and two chats asking about one decision are asking two different things. See
[[one-recommendation-one-decision]], [[a-relayed-approval-is-not-an-approval]],
[[questions-command]], [[communication-style-plain-and-brief]].
